# One Meaningful Game: Turn Your R36S/R36H Into a Dedicated Single-Game Console

Digital consumerism has transformed the way we experience video games. We have moved from owning games to constantly consuming them: new releases, updates, expansions, battle passes, seasonal events, endless libraries, and an ever-growing stream of content competing for our attention.

The problem is not simply that there are too many games. It is that we have too little time, attention, and awareness to truly experience them.

The pressure to keep up with the latest release creates a culture of urgency and disposability. Games are played, completed, abandoned, and quickly replaced by the next thing. Even retro gaming has become part of this cycle: enormous ROM collections, mini consoles, remastered editions, and subscription services offer us access to thousands of games, while ironically making it harder to form a meaningful connection with any of them.

This abundance creates a strange paradox: we have access to more games than any generation before us, yet we may be experiencing fewer of them in any meaningful sense.

In the rush to consume what is new, we risk forgetting why video games were created in the first place.

A video game is not simply a product, a service, or a piece of content to be consumed. It can be a window into another world — a place where we can explore ideas, emotions, stories, cultures, and perspectives that are different from our own. But it is also art.

And art is not meant to be consumed as quickly as possible.

Art asks us to slow down. To look closer. To listen. To reflect. To spend time with it.

Perhaps the most radical thing we can do in an age of digital abundance is simply to choose one game and give it our full attention. Not because it is the newest, the most popular, or the next item on a list — but because it deserves to be experienced.

Games should not only be consumed. They should be appreciated.
